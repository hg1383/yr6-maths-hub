🍕 What is a Fraction?

A fraction shows part of a whole. The whole could be a shape, an object, or a quantity — like a pizza, a number line, or a set of objects.

The two parts of a fraction Key Idea
34

💡 Memory trick: The Denominator is Down at the bottom. The Numerator is up at the North (top)!
⚠️ Important: Both parts must be equal for a fraction to make sense. Half a pizza means two equal halves — not just any two pieces!

✂️ Simplifying Fractions

Simplifying (also called "cancelling" or "reducing") means writing a fraction using the smallest possible numbers. The national curriculum calls this using common factors.

Using common factors to simplify NC Language
💡 Divide the numerator and denominator by a common factor — a number that divides into both exactly. Keep going until there are no more common factors. This gives you the simplest form.
Example 1 — Simplify 6/8
1
Common factors of 6 and 8: 1, 2. Use 2 (the highest common factor).
2
6 ÷ 2 = 3 and 8 ÷ 2 = 4
3
68 = 34 ✅ Simplest form
Example 2 — Simplify 12/20
1
Common factors of 12 and 20: 1, 2, 4. The highest is 4.
2
12 ÷ 4 = 3 and 20 ÷ 4 = 5
3
1220 = 35 ✅
🐌 Can't find the highest common factor? No problem — just divide by smaller common factors and keep going!
12/20 ÷ 2 = 6/10, then ÷ 2 again = 3/5 ✅ (Same answer — two steps instead of one)

⚖️ Ordering & Comparing Fractions

The national curriculum requires you to compare and order fractions — including fractions greater than 1. The key is making the denominators the same first.

Same denominator — easy! Method A
If the denominators are the same, just compare the numerators. Larger numerator = larger fraction.
Example
37 vs 57 → Same denominator → compare tops → 5 > 3 → 57 is bigger ✅
Different denominators — find a common denominator NC Method
💡 Find a number both denominators divide into. The national curriculum calls this the common denominator. Start with the simplest case: when one denominator is a multiple of the other.
Simple case — denominator is a multiple (Year 5 start)
12 vs 38
1

2

General case — find a common denominator (Year 6)

Ordering three fractions — put in order: 1/2, 2/5, 3/10

➕➖ Adding & Subtracting Fractions

The national curriculum teaches adding and subtracting fractions in two stages. Year 5 starts with fractions where one denominator is a multiple of the other. Year 6 extends this to any denominators.

Step 1 — Same denominator (simplest case) Year 4/5
Keep the denominator the same — only add or subtract the numerators.
Example: 2/7 + 3/7
27 + 37 = 57
Step 2 — One denominator is a multiple of the other Year 5
Example: 1/2 + 1/8

1
Convert ¾ to eighths: 3/4 = 6/8
2
6/8 − 1/8 = 5/8 ✅
Step 3 — Different denominators, find a common one Year 6
Example: 1/3 + 1/4

🔄 Mixed Numbers & Improper Fractions

A mixed number has a whole number part and a fraction part (like 2¾). An improper fraction has a numerator greater than (or equal to) the denominator (like 11/4). Converting between these is a key Year 5/6 skill.

Mixed number → Improper fraction Method
💡 Multiply the whole number by the denominator, then add the numerator. The denominator stays the same.
Example: Convert 2¾
1
Whole × denominator: 2 × 4 = 8
2
Add numerator: 8 + 3 = 11
3
Denominator stays: 114 ✅
🍕 Think of it as pizza: 2 whole pizzas cut into quarters = 8 slices, plus 3 more = 11 slices total → 11/4
Improper fraction → Mixed number Method
💡 Divide the numerator by the denominator. The quotient is the whole number, the remainder becomes the new numerator.
Example: Convert 17/5
1
17 ÷ 5 = 3 remainder 2
2
Whole = 3, remainder = 2, denominator stays 5
3
Answer: 325 ✅
Adding/subtracting mixed numbers Year 5/6
Example: 1¾ + 2½
1
Add whole numbers: 1 + 2 = 3
2
Add fractions: ¾ + ½ = 3/4 + 2/4 = 5/4 = 1¼
3
Combine: 3 + 1¼ = 4¼ ✅

✖️ Multiplying Fractions

Two types appear in SATs: fraction × whole number (Year 5, code 5F5) and fraction × fraction (Year 6, code 6F5a). Both come up regularly in the arithmetic paper.

Type 1 — Fraction × Whole Number Year 5 · 5F5

There are two ways to think about this. Both give the same answer — use whichever makes more sense to you.

Method A — Repeated Addition
Think of 3 × ⅖ as "three lots of ⅖". Just add the fraction three times.
⅖ + ⅖ + ⅖ = 6/5 = 1⅕ ✅

Why it works: multiplication is repeated addition. This is how it's first introduced in school — it builds on what you already know.
Method B — Multiply the Numerator
Multiply the top number by the whole number. The denominator stays the same. Simplify if needed.
3 × ⅖ → 3 × 2 = 6 → answer is 6/5 = 1⅕ ✅

Why it works: the denominator tells you the size of each piece (fifths). Multiplying by 3 just means you have 3× as many pieces.
Example A — 4 × ¾ using repeated addition
34 + 34 + 34 + 34 = 124 = 3 ✅
Example B — 4 × ¾ using multiply-the-numerator
4 × 34 → 4 × 3 = 12, denominator stays 4 → 124 = 3 ✅
Example C — 5 × ⅔ (answer needs converting to a mixed number)
5 × 23 → 5 × 2 = 10, denominator stays 3 → 103 = 3⅓ ✅
💡 If the answer is an improper fraction (top bigger than bottom), convert it to a mixed number: 10/3 = 3 remainder 1 = 3⅓.
Type 2 — Fraction × Fraction Year 6 · 6F5a

This appears in harder SATs questions. Again, two approaches — start with Method A.

Method A — Top × Top, Bottom × Bottom
Multiply the numerators together. Multiply the denominators together. Simplify the result.
¼ × ½ → 1×1=1, 4×2=8 → 1/8 ✅
Method B — Think "of" (use a bar model)
"¼ × ½" means "one quarter OF one half". Draw a half. Split that half into quarters. Each piece is 1/8 of the whole.
This visual approach helps when the calculation feels confusing.
Example — ⅖ × ⅚ (the answer simplifies — always check!)
1
Top × top: 2 × 5 = 10
2
Bottom × bottom: 5 × 6 = 30
3
10/30 → simplify (÷10) → 13 ✅
⚠️ Always simplify at the end. SATs mark schemes may accept 10/30 but examiners expect simplified answers.

➗ Dividing Fractions by Whole Numbers

The DfE national curriculum (code 6F5b) requires Year 6 pupils to divide a proper fraction by a whole number — for example ⅓ ÷ 2. Note: dividing a fraction by another fraction is NOT in KS2.

What does it actually mean? Start here
💡 Dividing by a whole number means splitting the fraction into that many equal groups.
½ ÷ 3 means "split one half into 3 equal pieces — how big is each piece?"

Think of it like this: if half a pizza is shared between 3 people, each person gets 1/6 of the whole pizza.
½ ÷ 3 — splitting the shaded half into 3 equal pieces:

Each teal piece = 1/6 of the whole bar. So ½ ÷ 3 = 1/6 ✅

How to calculate it Two methods — both work
Method A — Multiply the denominator (easiest for KS2)
Keep the numerator the same. Multiply the denominator by the whole number. Simplify if needed.
⅓ ÷ 2 → numerator stays 1, denominator: 3 × 2 = 6 → 1/6 ✅

Why it works: if thirds are split into 2, they become sixths (twice as many, twice as small).
Method B — Write the whole as a fraction, then multiply by its flip
⅓ ÷ 2 = ⅓ ÷ 2/1 = ⅓ × ½ = 1/6 ✅

Why it works: dividing by a number is the same as multiplying by its reciprocal (flipped version). This method connects to secondary school maths — useful to know but Method A is simpler for now.

💰 Fraction of an Amount

Finding a fraction of a quantity is one of the most tested fraction skills in SATs. The national curriculum describes this as using fractions as operators.

The method: divide by the denominator, multiply by the numerator NC Method
💡 ÷ denominator first, then × numerator.
This finds "one part" first, then scales up to how many parts you need.
Example 1: What is ¾ of 24?
1
Find ¼ first: 24 ÷ 4 = 6
2
Find ¾: 6 × 3 = 18
3
¾ of 24 = 18 ✅
Example 2: Working backwards — if ¼ of a length is 36 cm, what is the whole?
1
¼ = 36 cm, so multiply by 4 to find the whole
2
36 × 4 = 144 cm ✅
This is a Year 5/6 SATs favourite — "working backwards" from a unit fraction to find the whole.

🚀 Multiplying by 10, 100, 1000

When you multiply by a power of 10, the digits shift left — they don't get bigger, the place value does. This is where the magic zero comes in!

The digit-shift rule Magic Zero!
× 10 → digits move 1 place left
× 100 → digits move 2 places left
× 1000 → digits move 3 places left
🪄 The Magic Zero Trick: When multiplying a whole number by 10, 100 or 1000 — just add that many zeros to the end!
47 × 10 = 470  |  47 × 100 = 4,700  |  47 × 1000 = 47,000
⚠️ With decimals the magic zero doesn't work — the decimal point moves instead:
3.6 × 10 = 36  |  3.6 × 100 = 360  |  0.4 × 100 = 40

🚌 Bus Stop Method — Short Division

The bus stop method (short division) divides a number step by step, one digit at a time from left to right. Any leftover from each step gets carried to the next digit.

How it works Key Skill
Work left to right. Divide each digit. If there is a remainder, carry it to the next digit.
Example — 96 ÷ 4
1

2

🔄 Inverse Operations — Checking with the Inverse

Every operation has an opposite. Multiplication and division are inverses — you can use one to check the other. This is a key Year 6 skill.

What is the inverse? Key Concept
Multiplication and division are inverses of each other.
If 6 × 8 = 48  →  then 48 ÷ 8 = 6  AND  48 ÷ 6 = 8
How to use the inverse to check:
You worked out 252 ÷ 7 = 36.
Check: 36 × 7 = 252 ✅ — the inverse gives you the original number back.
Checking a division answer
1
You calculated: 192 ÷ 8 = 24
2
Check with inverse: 24 × 8 = 192 ✅
3
The answer matches — your division is correct!
💡 Always check division using multiplication. It takes 10 seconds and saves careless errors in tests.

👨‍👩‍👧‍👦 Fact Families

A fact family is a group of related multiplication and division facts that all use the same three numbers. Knowing one fact gives you three more for free.

The four facts in a family Key Idea
From the three numbers 6, 7 and 42 you get four facts:
6 × 7 = 42
7 × 6 = 42
42 ÷ 6 = 7
42 ÷ 7 = 6
💡 Scale them up! 6 × 7 = 42 → 60 × 7 = 420 → 420 ÷ 7 = 60 → 420 ÷ 60 = 7

Top tips for the arithmetic paper Exam Technique
✅ Show your working in the space provided — even if the final answer is wrong, method marks may be awarded.
⏱️ Don't spend too long on any one question. Skip and come back. 40 questions in 30 minutes = ~45 seconds each.
